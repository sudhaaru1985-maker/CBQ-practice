Case 1Sickle Cell Anaemia9-yr-old boy · African descent · HbS confirmed

A 9-year-old boy of African descent presents with recurrent episodes of severe limb pain and fatigue. His parents report that he becomes easily tired during physical activity. Physical examination reveals pallor and mild scleral icterus. Laboratory investigations show a haemoglobin level of 7.8 g/dL. Peripheral smear shows sickled cells and Howell-Jolly bodies. Haemoglobin electrophoresis confirms predominantly HbS.

Questions

1Describe THREE anatomic features of erythrocytes that contribute to the efficiency with which they transport oxygen.
✅ Model Answer
  1. Biconcave disc shape — increases the surface area-to-volume ratio, maximising gas exchange surface while shortening the diffusion distance to the cell interior.
  2. Absence of nucleus and organelles — maximises intracellular volume for haemoglobin (~280 million Hb molecules per RBC), directly increasing oxygen-carrying capacity.
  3. Flexible, deformable membrane — allows the RBC (7–8 µm) to squeeze through capillaries as narrow as 3 µm, ensuring O₂ delivery to peripheral tissues without rupturing.
2Outline the structure and oxygen-binding capacity of adult haemoglobin A (HbA).
✅ Model Answer

Structure: HbA is a tetramer of 2 α-globin + 2 β-globin chains (α₂β₂), each bound to one haem group (protoporphyrin IX with central Fe²⁺).

  • Each haem Fe²⁺ binds one O₂ → one HbA carries up to 4 O₂
  • Binding is cooperative (allosteric) → sigmoidal O₂ dissociation curve
  • T state (tense/deoxy — low affinity) ↔ R state (relaxed/oxy — high affinity)
HbA carries 1.34 mL O₂/g Hb. At Hb 15 g/dL → ~20 mL O₂/100 mL blood.
3Discuss the mechanism of oxygen transport by haemoglobin, and mention TWO haemoglobin variants that differ from HbA.
✅ Model Answer
  • In lungs (high pO₂, alkaline pH): O₂ binds haem Fe²⁺ → T→R shift → cooperative O₂ loading
  • In tissues (low pO₂, acidic pH): Bohr effect — H⁺ and CO₂ → R→T shift → O₂ released
  • 2,3-BPG stabilises T state → shifts curve right → promotes O₂ unloading

Two variants:

  • HbF (α₂γ₂) — lower 2,3-BPG affinity → higher O₂ affinity → transfers O₂ from maternal to foetal blood
  • HbS (α₂βS₂) — Glu→Val at β-chain codon 6 → polymerises under hypoxia → sickle cell disease
4Describe the specific genetic mutation that causes sickle cell anaemia and the inheritance pattern.
✅ Model Answer
  • Point mutation in codon 6 of the β-globin gene on chromosome 11: GAG → GTG
  • Amino acid change: Glutamic acid (Glu) → Valine (Val) at β-globin position 6
  • Valine is hydrophobic; deoxygenated HbS polymerises into rigid fibres → cell sickling
  • Autosomal recessive: HbSS = sickle cell disease; HbAS = sickle cell trait (carrier)
Both parents must be carriers (HbAS) for a 25% chance of an affected child per pregnancy.
5Explain why symptoms typically appear only after 6 months of age, and how HbF delays symptom onset.
✅ Model Answer
  • At birth, HbF (α₂γ₂) predominates (~70–90% of total Hb)
  • γ→β globin gene switching completes by ~6 months → HbS rises → sickling possible
  • HbF contains γ-chains (not β) → unaffected by βS mutation
  • HbF does not co-polymerise with HbS → inhibits polymer formation → sickling prevented while HbF is high
Rationale for hydroxyurea: reactivates HbF production in adults with SCD, reducing crises.
6Describe the pathogenesis of anaemia in sickle cell disease.
✅ Model Answer
  1. Extravascular haemolysis — rigid sickled cells trapped and phagocytosed by splenic macrophages → RBC lifespan reduced from 120 days to 10–20 days
  2. Intravascular haemolysis — repeated sickling/unsickling cycles → membrane damage → mechanical lysis → haemoglobinaemia

Bone marrow cannot compensate → chronic haemolytic anaemia. Compensatory reticulocytosis is present but insufficient.

Aplastic crisis (parvovirus B19) can cause sudden severe worsening by transiently suppressing erythropoiesis.
7Name and describe TWO peripheral blood smear findings in sickle cell disease.
✅ Model Answer
  1. Sickle cells (Drepanocytes) — elongated, crescent-shaped RBCs from polymerisation of deoxygenated HbS; irreversibly sickled cells remain deformed even after reoxygenation
  2. Howell-Jolly bodies — small basophilic nuclear remnants within RBCs; normally removed by the spleen; indicate splenic hypofunction/autosplenectomy
Other findings: target cells, polychromasia (reticulocytosis), nucleated RBCs.
8Name ONE acute and ONE chronic complication of sickle cell disease and briefly describe their pathophysiology.
✅ Model Answer

Acute — Vaso-occlusive Crisis: Triggers (hypoxia, dehydration, cold) → HbS polymerises → rigid sickled cells obstruct microvascular circulation → tissue ischaemia → severe pain (bones, chest, abdomen)

Chronic — Avascular Necrosis (femoral head): Repeated vaso-occlusion in terminal end-arteries → ischaemic infarction of bone → structural collapse → chronic pain, joint destruction

Other acute: acute chest syndrome, stroke, splenic sequestration. Other chronic: renal failure, pulmonary hypertension, retinopathy.

Case 2β-Thalassaemia Major6-yr-old · Mediterranean · Absent HbA

A 6-year-old child of Mediterranean descent presents with pallor, fatigue, and delayed growth. On examination, there is frontal bossing and hepatosplenomegaly. A complete blood count shows low haemoglobin levels. Peripheral blood smear shows microcytic hypochromic red cells with target cells. Haemoglobin electrophoresis reveals elevated HbF and HbA₂ with absent HbA.

Questions

1Explain the genetic defect in β-thalassaemia and its biochemical consequences.
✅ Model Answer

Mutations in the β-globin gene (chromosome 11) — point mutations affecting promoter, splice sites, or polyadenylation signals.

  • β⁰: completely absent β-chain production
  • β⁺: reduced β-chain production
  • Major: homozygous β⁰/β⁰ or β⁰/β⁺

Biochemical consequences:

  • Excess free α-chains accumulate → precipitate in erythroblasts → membrane damage → ineffective erythropoiesis
  • Compensatory ↑ HbF (α₂γ₂) and ↑ HbA₂ (α₂δ₂); HbA absent or severely reduced
2Describe the pathogenesis of anaemia in β-thalassaemia major.
✅ Model Answer
  1. Ineffective erythropoiesis — α-chain precipitates destroy erythroblasts in bone marrow before maturation → greatly reduced functional RBC output
  2. Haemolytic anaemia — RBCs with α-chain inclusions → membrane damage → premature splenic destruction
  3. Compensatory extramedullary haematopoiesis — marrow expands into cortical bone (frontal bossing, "hair-on-end" skull X-ray) and liver/spleen (hepatosplenomegaly)
Anaemia is so severe that regular transfusions (every 2–4 weeks) are required, leading to secondary iron overload.
3List TWO peripheral blood smear findings characteristic of thalassaemia.
✅ Model Answer
  1. Microcytic hypochromic red cells — small RBCs (low MCV) with enlarged central pallor; deficient β-chains → less HbA → reduced haemoglobin per cell
  2. Target cells (Codocytes) — central dense area + pale ring + dense outer rim; increased membrane surface area relative to Hb content
Additional: nucleated RBCs, teardrops, basophilic stippling (α-chain inclusions), polychromasia.

2List FOUR conditions that can lead to thrombocytopenia.
✅ Model Answer

Decreased production:

  1. Aplastic anaemia — bone marrow failure → reduced megakaryocytes → decreased platelet production
  2. Leukaemia / bone marrow infiltration — neoplastic cells crowd out normal haematopoiesis

Increased destruction:

  1. ITP — IgG autoantibodies coat platelets → phagocytosis by splenic macrophages
  2. DIC — systemic coagulation → platelet consumption and destruction
Additional: TTP, hypersplenism, drug-induced (heparin → HIT), sepsis.
3List the conditions with abnormal platelet function and normal platelet count.
✅ Model Answer

Inherited: Glanzmann's thrombasthenia (deficiency of GpIIb/IIIa); Bernard-Soulier syndrome (deficiency of GpIb-IX-V); Von Willebrand disease (reduced/abnormal vWF)

Acquired: Aspirin/NSAIDs (irreversible COX-1 inhibition → ↓TXA₂); Uraemia (guanidinosuccinic acid impairs function); Myeloproliferative disorders (qualitative defects despite elevated count)

5Describe the coagulation cascade.
✅ Model Answer

Extrinsic pathway: Tissue factor (TF) + Factor VII → VIIa. TF-VIIa complex activates Factor X → Xa.

Intrinsic pathway: XII → XIIa → XI → XIa → IXa. IXa + VIIIa (tenase complex) + Ca²⁺ + phospholipid → Factor X → Xa.

Common pathway: Xa + Va (prothrombinase complex) + Ca²⁺ → Prothrombin (II) → Thrombin (IIa) → Fibrinogen → Fibrin. Factor XIIIa crosslinks fibrin → stable clot.

PT tests extrinsic + common (VII, X, V, II, I). aPTT tests intrinsic + common (XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, I).
6What is the immune abnormality or hypersensitivity in ITP?
✅ Model Answer

Type II Hypersensitivity — Antibody-mediated cytotoxicity

  • Viral infection → molecular mimicry or polyclonal B-cell activation
  • IgG autoantibodies against platelet surface glycoproteins, principally GpIIb/IIIa (and sometimes GpIb-IX)
  • IgG-coated platelets → FcγRIII receptors on splenic macrophages → phagocytic destruction
  • Complement (C3b) may also contribute; antibodies may cross-react with megakaryocytes → impaired production
In children: acute and self-limiting post-viral. In adults: chronic with persistent autoantibody production.
7Describe the mechanisms of autoimmunity development in patients.
✅ Model Answer
  1. Molecular mimicry — viral antigen resembles self-antigen → cross-reactive immune response
  2. Failure of central tolerance — autoreactive T and B cells not eliminated in thymus/bone marrow
  3. Failure of peripheral tolerance — defective Tregs fail to suppress autoreactive lymphocytes; loss of anergy
  4. Polyclonal B-cell activation — viral superantigens activate many B-cell clones including autoreactive ones
  5. Epitope spreading — tissue damage exposes cryptic epitopes → autoimmune response expands
  6. Bystander activation — inflammatory APCs present self-antigens with co-stimulatory signals to autoreactive T cells

Case 4Multiple Myeloma67-yr-old man · Back pain · M-spike on SPE

A 67-year-old man presents with persistent lower back pain, fatigue, and recurrent respiratory infections. On examination there is pallor, no lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly. Skeletal examination reveals tenderness over the lumbar spine. Based on serum protein electrophoresis and bone marrow study, he is diagnosed with multiple myeloma. Immunotherapy was decided to be a therapeutic option.

Questions

1List TWO findings most likely on bone marrow aspiration/biopsy in this patient.
✅ Model Answer
  1. Plasma cell infiltration (>10%, typically >30%) — neoplastic plasma cells with eccentric nucleus, clock-face chromatin, prominent perinuclear hof (Golgi zone), basophilic cytoplasm
  2. Rouleaux formation — RBCs stacked like coins due to elevated M-protein altering electrostatic repulsion
Additional: Russell bodies (intracytoplasmic Ig globules), flame cells (IgA myeloma), Dutcher bodies (intranuclear Ig inclusions).
2List the possible complications that are likely to occur in this patient.
✅ Model Answer

CRAB criteria:

  • C — HyperCalcaemia: osteolysis → Ca²⁺ release → confusion, polyuria, constipation
  • R — Renal failure: light chain (Bence Jones) cast nephropathy; also hypercalcaemia nephropathy, amyloidosis
  • A — Anaemia: bone marrow infiltration → suppressed erythropoiesis
  • B — Bone disease: osteolytic lesions → pathological fractures, vertebral collapse → back pain

Additional: Recurrent infections (immunoparesis); Hyperviscosity syndrome (excess M-protein); AL Amyloidosis (light chain deposition)

3What is the mechanism of osteolytic lesions in the bone in multiple myeloma?
✅ Model Answer

Osteoclast activation ↑↑:

  • Myeloma cells secrete RANKL → osteoclast differentiation and activation
  • Also secrete IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, MIP-1α → further osteoclastogenesis
  • OPG (natural RANKL inhibitor) is reduced by myeloma cells

Osteoblast suppression ↓↓:

  • Myeloma cells secrete DKK-1 → inhibits Wnt signalling → blocks osteoblast differentiation → impaired bone formation

Result: "punched-out" lytic lesions, hypercalcaemia, pathological fractures.

Myeloma bone lesions show NO uptake on bone scan — osteoblasts are suppressed. Use plain X-ray or CT.
4Explain how serum protein electrophoresis (SPE) can be used to diagnose multiple myeloma.
✅ Model Answer

Serum proteins are separated by electrical charge. Normal pattern: albumin, α1, α2, β, and broad polyclonal γ-globulin.

  • In myeloma: a narrow, tall, discrete "M-spike" appears in the β or γ-globulin region
  • Represents monoclonal immunoglobulin (M-protein) from the neoplastic plasma cell clone (identical Ig, same heavy and light chain type)
  • Most commonly IgG (~50%) or IgA (~20%)
  • Normal polyclonal Ig are suppressed (immunoparesis)
  • Quantifying M-protein monitors disease burden and treatment response
UPEP detects free light chains (Bence Jones protein — κ or λ). Serum FLC assay provides sensitive detection.
5Which immune cell is affected in multiple myeloma?
✅ Model Answer

Plasma cells — terminally differentiated B lymphocytes that have undergone class-switch recombination and somatic hypermutation in germinal centres.

  • A single plasma cell clone undergoes malignant transformation → proliferates uncontrollably in bone marrow
  • All cells secrete identical (monoclonal) immunoglobulin (M-protein)
  • Malignant clone suppresses normal polyclonal plasma cells → immunoparesis → recurrent infections
Progression: MGUS → Smouldering Myeloma → Active Myeloma

Case 5Haemorrhagic Disease of Newborn (VKDB)5-day-old neonate · Home birth · No vitamin K prophylaxis

A 5-day-old male newborn is brought to the emergency department due to bleeding from the umbilical stump and fresh blood in the stool. He was born at home, is exclusively breastfed, and did not receive vitamin K prophylaxis at birth. The baby is diagnosed with haemorrhagic disease of the newborn and is treated with vitamin K.

Questions

1List the vitamin K–dependent clotting factors and describe their contribution to the coagulation cascade. (3 marks)3 marks
✅ Model Answer

Mnemonic: "1972" + Protein C & S

FactorRole in Coagulation
Factor II (Prothrombin)Common pathway: converted to thrombin (IIa) → cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin
Factor VIIExtrinsic pathway: TF-VIIa complex activates Factor X. Shortest half-life (~4–6 hrs) → falls earliest
Factor IXIntrinsic pathway: IXa + VIIIa (tenase complex) activates Factor X; deficiency = Haemophilia B

Vitamin K enables γ-carboxylation (Glu→Gla) — essential for Ca²⁺ binding and phospholipid membrane attachment of these factors.
2Outline the three major steps of haemostasis. (3 marks)3 marks
✅ Model Answer
  1. Primary Haemostasis: vascular injury → collagen exposed + vWF released → platelet adhesion (vWF–GpIb) → activation (ADP, TXA₂) → aggregation (GpIIb/IIIa + fibrinogen) → primary platelet plug
  2. Secondary Haemostasis: tissue factor → coagulation cascade → thrombin → fibrinogen → fibrin; Factor XIIIa crosslinks fibrin → stable clot
  3. Fibrinolysis: tPA → plasminogen → plasmin → degrades fibrin → FDPs including D-dimer; limits clot size and restores vessel patency

4Describe the mechanism of action of vitamin K in the treatment of this newborn baby. (3 marks)3 marks
✅ Model Answer
  1. Vitamin K (reduced form, KH₂) is cofactor for γ-glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX) in hepatocyte endoplasmic reticulum
  2. GGCX adds –COOH to specific glutamic acid (Glu) residues → γ-carboxyglutamic acid (Gla) on VK-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X, Protein C, S)
  3. Gla residues chelate Ca²⁺ ions → factors bind phospholipid membranes (activated platelet surfaces) → assembly of procoagulant complexes (tenase, prothrombinase) → coagulation proceeds
  4. Without VK: Glu remains uncarboxylated → PIVKAs (non-functional factors) → coagulopathy
VK cycle: KH₂ oxidised → VK epoxide → recycled by VKOR. Warfarin blocks VKOR — same mechanism.
5State the type of vitamin K for this newborn baby. (1 mark)1 mark
✅ Model Answer

Vitamin K₁ (Phytomenadione / Phytonadione) — single IM injection of 1 mg at birth (within 6 hours). IM route preferred for reliable absorption and long-acting hepatic storage.

K₂ (Menaquinone) = gut bacteria. K₃ (Menadione) = synthetic — NOT used in neonates (risk of haemolysis, jaundice).
6List TWO adverse effects of vitamin K. (2 marks)2 marks
✅ Model Answer
  1. Anaphylactoid/anaphylactic reactions — primarily with IV administration; caused by solubilising agents (polyoxyethylated castor oil) → flushing, hypotension, bronchospasm; give IV slowly
  2. Pain, swelling, bruising at injection site — minor local reaction following IM administration; common but self-limiting
7Describe the prophylactic treatment given to newborns to prevent such condition. (2 marks)2 marks
✅ Model Answer
  • Vitamin K₁ (Phytomenadione) 1 mg IM at birth (within 6 hours) — recommended for all newborns by WHO and national guidelines
  • Single IM dose prevents early, classic, and late VKDB (protection up to 6 months)

Rationale: Neonates born with low VK stores (poor placental transfer) + sterile gut (no VK₂-producing bacteria) + breast milk low in VK (~2 µg/L)

Oral alternative: 3 doses of VK₁ 2 mg at birth, 1 week, and 4–6 weeks — less reliable in breastfed infants; not for malabsorption or liver disease.

Case 6Iron Deficiency Anaemia25-yr-old woman · Heavy periods · Koilonychia

A 25-year-old female presents with a 6-month history of progressive fatigue, exertional breathlessness, and generalised weakness. She reports heavy menstrual periods for the past 2 years, with cycles lasting 7–8 days. She has two children and follows a predominantly vegetarian diet. On examination she is pale, with angular cheilitis and koilonychia. Based on laboratory investigations, she is diagnosed with iron deficiency anaemia.

Questions

1State the stage at which iron is incorporated in the RBCs.
✅ Model Answer

Iron is incorporated at the normoblast (erythroblast) stage in the bone marrow:

  • Transferrin-Fe³⁺ binds transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) → receptor-mediated endocytosis
  • Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺ by STEAP3 (ferrireductase) in acidic endosome
  • Fe²⁺ enters mitochondria via mitoferrin (SLC25A37)
  • Fe²⁺ inserted into protoporphyrin IX by ferrochelatase → haem
  • Haem + globin chains → haemoglobin
Most active at polychromatophilic and orthochromatic normoblast stages, before nucleus extrusion.
2List the possible causes of iron deficiency anaemia.
✅ Model Answer

1. Increased blood/iron loss (most common): Menorrhagia (most common in premenopausal women); GI bleeding (peptic ulcer, colorectal carcinoma, hookworm, haemorrhoids)

2. Inadequate dietary intake: Vegetarian/vegan diet (haem iron 2–3× more bioavailable than non-haem); poverty, malnutrition; cow's milk-only infants

3. Malabsorption: Coeliac disease, Crohn's disease; post-gastrectomy (↓gastric acid → impaired Fe³⁺→Fe²⁺ reduction); H. pylori

4. Increased demand: Pregnancy, lactation, rapid growth in infancy/adolescence

3State the peripheral smear findings in this patient.
✅ Model Answer
  • Microcytic hypochromic red cells — MCV <80 fL, enlarged central pallor (>1/3 diameter); insufficient Hb synthesis
  • Pencil cells (cigar/elliptocytes) — elongated, pencil-shaped RBCs; characteristic of IDA
  • Anisocytosis — variation in RBC size (elevated RDW >14%)
  • Poikilocytosis — variation in RBC shape
  • Target cells — may be present
  • Reduced reticulocyte count — iron-limited erythropoiesis

6Identify the rate-limiting enzyme of haem synthesis. Name its cofactor and explain how haem regulates this enzyme.
✅ Model Answer

Rate-limiting enzyme: ALA Synthase (ALAS)

  • Reaction: Succinyl-CoA + Glycine → ALA (Step 1, mitochondria)
  • Cofactor: Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP) — active form of Vitamin B6

Haem regulation (negative feedback — 3 mechanisms):

  • Transcriptional repression: haem represses ALAS gene transcription → less enzyme
  • Mitochondrial import blockade: haem inhibits ALAS precursor translocation into mitochondria
  • Direct allosteric inhibition: haem allosterically inhibits ALAS activity
ALAS2 (erythroid-specific): also regulated by iron via IRP/IRE system — iron deficiency → IRP binds IRE on ALAS2 mRNA → translational suppression → less haem synthesis in RBCs.
🔬 Biochemistry
7Describe the specific step at which iron (Fe²⁺) is incorporated into the haem ring. Name the enzyme involved and the form of iron required.
✅ Model Answer

Step 8 — Final step of haem synthesis:

  • Enzyme: Ferrochelatase (haem synthase) — inner mitochondrial membrane
  • Reaction: Protoporphyrin IX + Fe²⁺ → Haem
  • Fe²⁺ inserted into centre of porphyrin ring by displacing two H⁺ from pyrrole nitrogens

Form of iron required: Fe²⁺ (ferrous) — NOT Fe³⁺ (ferric)

  • Plasma transport: Fe³⁺ bound to transferrin
  • After endocytosis: Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺ by STEAP3 in endosome
  • Fe²⁺ → mitochondria via mitoferrin (SLC25A37) → ferrochelatase
Ferrochelatase also inhibited by lead (Pb) → FEP/ZPP accumulates → biomarker of lead poisoning.
🔬 Biochemistry
8Explain what happens to haem synthesis in iron deficiency anaemia and how this is reflected in laboratory investigations.
✅ Model Answer
  • Insufficient Fe²⁺ → ferrochelatase cannot insert iron into protoporphyrin IX
  • Protoporphyrin IX accumulates → zinc substituted → zinc protoporphyrin (ZPP) formed
  • IDA → IRP binds IRE on ALAS2 mRNA → translational suppression → less ALA synthase → further ↓ haem synthesis
  • Less haem → less Hb per RBC → microcytic hypochromic erythrocytes

Laboratory reflections:

  • ↑ FEP / ZPP — marker of blocked ferrochelatase
  • ↓ MCV, ↓ MCH, ↓ MCHC, ↓ Hb — microcytic hypochromic anaemia
  • ↓ Ferritin, ↓ Serum iron, ↑ TIBC
